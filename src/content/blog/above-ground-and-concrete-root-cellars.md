An above ground root cellar holds produce in a mound, a stack of sandbags, or a shed, so you never have to dig. A poured concrete version goes into a hillside or tucks into a basement corner. Either one works if you hit the storage conditions: 32 to 40 degrees Fahrenheit and 85 to 95 percent humidity, per The Old Farmer's Almanac.

Below you'll find materials and rough costs, the build steps for each style, the mistakes that ruin a crop, and a comparison table. For a poured build, see [How to Build a Root Cellar](https://harvestmap.co/blog/diy-root-cellar/).

## What You Need (and What It Actually Costs)

For a concrete root cellar, gather concrete, footers, rebar, plastic sheeting, plywood forms, insulation, an insulated door, and cinder blocks if you're building stairs. An 8 x 8-foot cellar takes roughly 350 cinder blocks for the walls, according to ShunTool. One large project used about 21 cubic yards of concrete. Wire 10mm rebar in a 2 foot grid pattern, with extra around the door, per A Traditional Life.

An above ground root cellar or no-dig build swaps most of that for sandbags, straw, dried leaves, and a thick wooden lid. A pit-style version uses a garbage can or similar container. Dig a hole slightly larger than the diameter of the can, deep enough that the lid sits 4 inches above the soil level, per The Old Farmer's Almanac.

Cost varies more than any other part of this project. Concrete, equipment rental, and site work drive that spread. Budget for an insulated floor hatch or entry door, and pick up reference books such as *Build Your Own Underground Root Cellar* or *The Complete Root Cellar Book*.

## Step-by-Step: Building It

Pick the style before you order anything. Above-ground root cellars use a mound, sandbags, or a shed. A concrete root cellar gets poured into a hillside or set into a basement corner. That choice drives every measurement that follows.

### Site, Dig, Form, Pour

For a precast unit, dig a hole 15 feet long by 9 feet wide with a maximum bury of 2 feet, per Bonner Concrete specifications. For a pit cellar, dig a hole slightly larger than your container and deep enough that the lid sits 4 inches above soil level, as the Old Farmer's Almanac advises. If your winter temperatures drop below 25°F, dig deep enough that all crops sit below the soil's surface.

Complete forms for all four walls with rebar and block-outs for the door and vents. Wire 10mm rebar in a 2-foot grid pattern, with extra reinforcement around the door. Bend rebar up into the roof forms to tie wall and roof together.

### Ventilation, Access, and Finish

Add two 4-inch vent holes and an insulated exterior-grade access door. For a basement build, plan 4-foot footings with dirt backfill. Packed earth is the preferred flooring for outdoor cellars. Set shelves 1 to 3 inches away from the walls so air can circulate and mold stays down.

A basement slab alone won't work. A bare slab gives you no enclosed, vented air pocket, so it can't hold the cool, humid conditions produce needs. Build the room.

## Mistakes to Avoid

### Ventilation and water

Skip ventilation and you lose the crop. Without intake and exhaust vents, ethylene gas spoils produce and mold takes hold. For a basement retrofit, plan 4 foot footings and dirt backfill.

Drainage decides whether a pit or hillside cellar survives its first wet winter. Dig only where soil is sandy and well draining, and use an elevated slope so water runs away from the pit as it moves downhill. If your ground is heavy clay, don't dig there. Build a mound or sandbag cellar above grade instead. Digging deep does give you stable temperatures, but a flooded pit stores nothing.

### Depth, air gaps and the slab question

In cold climates, shallow digging freezes the crop. If winter temperatures drop below 25°F, dig deep enough that all crops sit under the soil's surface. Temperature stability isn't complete until about 10 feet deep, so treat any shallower pit as a cold-climate compromise.

Shelves pushed flat against walls kill circulation and invite airborne mold. Keep shelves 1 to 3 inches away from the walls.

A concrete slab alone gives you no cool, humid air pocket. You need an enclosed, vented space to make it work like a cellar. Skip the bare slab.

Before you pour, confirm permits and structural support. A large build can take about 21 cubic yards of concrete, so a failed inspection is expensive.

## FAQ

### How cold does a 'root cellar' have to be? Can it be a few degrees warmer than just above freezing?

A working root cellar holds 32 to 40 degrees Fahrenheit and 85 to 95 percent humidity, according to the Old Farmer's Almanac. Missouri Extension lists 32 to 40 degrees Fahrenheit for most stored crops. A few degrees warmer than just above freezing sits inside that band, so yes, it can work. Warm-weather crops are the exception. Keep sweet potatoes at 50 to 55 degrees Fahrenheit and never let them drop below 50 degrees Fahrenheit, and hold green tomatoes above 50 degrees Fahrenheit too. Onions and garlic tolerate drier air than the 80 to 95 percent humidity most produce needs.

### What are some alternatives to a root cellar?

A basement corner, a buried garbage can, or a sandbag structure can all do the job. Add an insulated exterior grade access door from the basement and two 4 inch vent holes to make a basement corner work. For a buried can, dig a hole slightly larger than the can's diameter and deep enough that the lid sits 4 inches above the soil level. A childhood farm shed used a mound of earth piled up to 3ft over buried root vegetables. For a no-dig option, see Cellar Storage Ideas for Any House.

### How to build a cheap root cellar?

That range covers the whole spectrum from a buried can to a poured concrete room. Dig a hole, flare the sides so it doesn't cave in, line it with straw and dried leaves, then cover it with a thick wooden lid and soil. Packed earth is the preferred flooring for outdoor cellars. For a full walkthrough, read [How to Build a Root Cellar](https://harvestmap.co/blog/diy-root-cellar/).

### How long will vegetables stay good in a root cellar?

Storage life depends on the crop and on hitting its temperature and humidity band. Missouri Extension gives 32 to 40 degrees Fahrenheit and 90 to 95 percent humidity for the crops that keep longest, and 32 to 35 degrees Fahrenheit ideal for the coldest keepers. Get the numbers wrong and the loss is fast. Check your thermometer and hygrometer weekly rather than trusting the room to hold steady on its own.
