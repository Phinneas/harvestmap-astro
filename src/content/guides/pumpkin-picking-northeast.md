Pumpkin picking is the centerpiece of the Northeast fall. From late September through Halloween, farms across New York, Massachusetts, Vermont, and Maine open their patches to families looking for the perfect jack-o'-lantern, a pie pumpkin, or just an afternoon in the crisp autumn air. Here is how to do it right.

## When to go

Pumpkin season in the Northeast runs from **late September through October**, with peak picking in the first three weeks of October.

Pumpkins are ready when they are fully colored and the rind is hard. Most patches open by the last weekend of September and run daily through Halloween. The best selection is in early to mid-October — by the final week, the big carving pumpkins are often picked over.

If you want a specific variety — a white Lumina, a warty Knucklehead, or a big prize pumpkin — go early. Specialty varieties sell out first.

Weather matters less for pumpkins than for berries or stone fruit, but a wet October can leave the patch muddy. Wear boots if it has rained. And go early in the day on weekends: the most popular farms in New York's Hudson Valley and the Berkshire region get crowded by mid-morning.

## What to bring

- **Layers.** October in the Northeast can be 70 degrees or 40 degrees. A warm morning turns cold fast once the sun drops.
- **Boots or sturdy shoes.** Patches are dirt and grass, often muddy after autumn rain. Sneakers will get dirty.
- **A wagon or wheelbarrow.** Pumpkins are heavy. Many farms lend or rent wagons, but if you have your own, bring it. Carrying a 20-pound pumpkin across a field is harder than it sounds.
- **Gloves.** Pumpkin stems are prickly and the vines can scratch. A pair of garden gloves saves your hands.
- **Cash.** Some farm stands are cash-only, especially for smaller purchases.
- **A blanket or tarp for your car.** Pumpkins are dirty. A tarp in the trunk saves your interior.

## Choosing a patch

Not all pumpkin operations are the same. Some things to check before you go:

**Patch vs. pre-picked.** Some farms sell pumpkins from a bin but do not have a u-pick patch. If walking the field matters to you, confirm the farm offers it.

**Variety selection.** The best patches grow a range of sizes and colors — classic orange, white, warty, miniature, and heirlooms. Check the farm's website or call to see what they grow.

**Activities beyond pumpkins.** Many Northeast farms combine pumpkin patches with corn mazes, apple orchards, hayrides, cider donuts, and farm stores. If you are making a day of it, these extras matter. The big destination farms book up on October weekends — reserve tickets online if required.

**Photo opportunities.** If photos are part of the plan, look for farms with scenic setups — old trucks piled with pumpkins, sunflower backdrops, or mountain views.

## Which varieties to look for

Northeast pumpkin patches grow more than the standard orange globe. The ones you are most likely to find:

**Howden** is the classic jack-o'-lantern pumpkin. Deep orange, ribbed, and thick-walled. If you want a traditional carving pumpkin, this is it.

**Connecticut Field** is an heirloom variety and one of the oldest American pumpkins. Large, flat, and slightly irregular. Good for carving and for livestock feed.

**Sugar Pie** (also called New England Pie) is the pumpkin to pick if you want to bake. Small, dense, and sweet-fleshed. One Sugar Pie makes a pie. Do not try to carve a pie from a carving pumpkin — the flesh is watery and bland.

**Lumina** is a white pumpkin. Ghostly and modern. Carves well and makes a striking display. The flesh is also edible.

**Knucklehead** and **Warty Goblin** are novelty varieties covered in bumps. They look like something out of a fairy tale. Kids love them.

**Mini pumpkins** (Jack-Be-Little, Wee-B-Little) are palm-sized and last for months on a tabletop.

## How to pick

A ripe pumpkin has a hard rind. Press your thumbnail into the skin — if it dents easily, the pumpkin is not ready. The stem should be dry and firm, not green and flexible.

Cut the pumpkin from the vine with pruning shears or a sharp knife, leaving 3-4 inches of stem. Do not carry the pumpkin by the stem — it can snap off, and a pumpkin without a stem decays faster. Lift the pumpkin from the bottom.

Look for pumpkins free of soft spots, cuts, and bruises. A flat spot on the bottom is fine and actually helps the pumpkin sit upright for carving. Avoid any pumpkin with a soft spot anywhere — it will rot within a week.

## After the pick

Uncarved pumpkins keep for weeks, even months, if stored right. Keep them in a cool, dry place — a porch, garage, or unheated room works. Do not let them freeze. Avoid direct sun, which fades the color and softens the rind. A sound pumpkin stored properly will last through Thanksgiving.

Once carved, a jack-o'-lantern lasts 3-7 days before collapsing. Carve close to Halloween if you want it to look good on the night. To extend its life, smear petroleum jelly on the cut edges and keep it in the refrigerator during the day.

For cooking: roast Sugar Pie pumpkins whole or halved at 375 degrees until the flesh is soft, then scoop and puree. Homemade pumpkin puree freezes well and beats canned for flavor.
