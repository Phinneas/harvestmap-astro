Blackberries are a Southern summer staple. Wild brambles line the roadsides and fence rows from Georgia to the Carolinas, and u-pick farms have turned the tradition into a reliable family outing. The Southeast's long, hot summers and plentiful rain produce berries that are juicier and more aromatic than anything you will find in a supermarket. Here is how to make the most of blackberry season.

## When to go

Blackberry season in the Southeast runs from **mid-June through July**, with peak picking in late June and the first two weeks of July.

The season starts earliest in southern Georgia and the coastal plain, where farms may open by mid-June. The Carolinas, Tennessee, and the Piedmont region follow a week or two later. Higher elevations in the Appalachian foothills can run into early August.

Blackberries are less weather-sensitive than strawberries, but a prolonged drought can shrink the berries and an overly wet June can make them mushy. Call ahead to check on crop conditions, especially after a stretch of heavy rain.

## What to bring

- **Long pants and a long-sleeved shirt.** Blackberry canes have thorns. Even thornless varieties can scratch. Lightweight, breathable fabric is best in the Southern heat.
- **Sturdy closed-toe shoes.** Rows are dirt and grass, often muddy after summer thunderstorms.
- **Sun hat and sunscreen.** Most blackberry fields are open and unshaded, and the July sun is intense.
- **Containers.** Farms usually provide buckets, but bringing your own shallow containers helps prevent crushing. Do not pile berries more than three or four deep.
- **Bug spray.** Mosquitoes and chiggers are common in the Southeast during summer. Apply before you enter the field.
- **Water.** Southern humidity makes dehydration sneak up on you. Bring more than you think you need.
- **Cash.** Some smaller farms are cash-only or offer a discount for cash.

## Which varieties to look for

Southeast blackberry farms grow a mix of thornless and thorned varieties. The ones you are most likely to find:

**Navaho** is a thornless variety and a Southeast favorite. Medium-sized, sweet, with a classic blackberry flavor. The canes are upright and easy to pick. Good for fresh eating and freezing.

**Ouachita** is another thornless workhorse. The berries are large, firm, and sweet. If you are picking for jam or cobbler, Ouachita holds its shape well when cooked.

**Arapaho** is one of the earliest thornless varieties. Smaller berries but very sweet and aromatic. Often the first to ripen on a farm.

**Kiowa** is a thorned variety that produces enormous berries — some as long as your thumb. The size makes it popular for u-pick, though the thorns mean you need to be careful. Worth the trouble if you want show-stopping berries.

## How to pick

Blackberries do not ripen after picking, so pick only berries that are fully black and dull (not shiny). A shiny blackberry is underripe and will be tart. A deep, dull black with a slight give when you press gently is what you want.

The easiest method: cup the berry in your fingers and roll it gently toward you. A ripe blackberry releases from the plant with almost no effort. If you have to pull, it is not ready. The receptacle — the white core inside the berry — should come with the fruit, leaving a clean stem behind.

Pick into shallow containers. Blackberries crush easily under their own weight. A deep bucket will turn the bottom layer to mush by the time you walk back to the stand.

## After the pick

Blackberries are the most perishable of the common berries. Plan to use them within 24-48 hours. Do not wash them until you are ready to eat — moisture speeds decay.

Store them unwashed in the refrigerator, spread in a single layer on a paper towel inside a container. Check for any crushed or moldy berries and remove them immediately. One bad berry will spread mold to the rest in a day.

For freezing: spread unwashed berries in a single layer on a baking sheet, freeze until solid, then transfer to a bag. Frozen blackberries are good for smoothies, cobblers, and jam for up to a year. Rinse them after pulling from the freezer, not before.

For jam and preserves: blackberries are high in pectin and natural sugar, which makes them ideal for canning. A simple blackberry jam needs little more than berries, sugar, and lemon juice. If you are picking for canning, aim for 4-6 pounds per batch.
