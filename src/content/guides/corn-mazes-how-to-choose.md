A good corn maze turns a field of corn into an afternoon of wandering, dead ends, and — if you are unlucky — a genuine question of whether you will ever find the exit. They open in late summer, peak through October, and shut down with the first hard frost. Here is how to pick one worth your time.

## When they open

Most corn mazes open between **mid-September and early October**, though a handful in warmer states open as early as August. The maze itself is planted in the spring, then cut into the corn once the stalks are tall enough — usually 6 to 8 feet by late summer.

The season runs until the corn is cut down or killed by frost, which in most of the country means early November. Many farms run the maze on weekends only early in the season, then switch to daily hours through October. Always check the farm's hours before you go — nothing is more disappointing than driving out to a maze that closed at dusk.

## How they work

Most mazes are **not** just random paths. They are cut into a design — a tractor, a football logo, a giant pumpkin — that you can only see from the air. On the ground, you get a map, a set of checkpoints, and sometimes a trivia question at each station to keep you moving in the right direction.

Two common styles:

**The check-in maze.** You collect stamps or answer questions at numbered stations. This gives the maze structure and lets the farm track how far you got. If you want the full experience, do the stations in order.

**The escape maze.** No map, no checkpoints — just find your way out. These are shorter but more disorienting. Good if you have kids with a short attention span or if you actually want to feel lost.

## Choosing a maze

Look past the size. A 10-acre maze is not automatically better than a 3-acre one — it is just longer. What matters is the design and the extras.

**Difficulty.** Family mazes are short, wide paths designed to be done in 20-30 minutes. Adult or "haunted" mazes are longer, narrower, and sometimes dark. Ask which one you are signing up for, especially if you are bringing kids.

**Terrain.** Corn mazes are dirt. After rain they are mud. Check whether the path is stroller-friendly if you are bringing one.

**Extras.** Most farms pair a corn maze with pumpkin picking, hayrides, a cider mill, or a farm stand. If you are making a day of it, pick a farm that has enough to do after the maze.

## What to bring

- **Sturdy shoes.** Corn maze paths are uneven dirt with roots and leftover stalks. Sneakers at minimum, boots if it rained.
- **Water.** You can be in a large maze for an hour or more. There is no water station in the middle of the field.
- **A charged phone.** For the map, the time, and — let's be honest — the flashlight app if it gets late.
- **A paper map.** Farms hand them out for a reason. Phones die and GPS is useless in a field of corn.
- **A buddy.** M.azes are more fun with someone to blame for the wrong turns.

## Night mazes

Many farms run their mazes **after dark** on October weekends, lit by flashlight or glow sticks. These are a different animal — colder, spookier, and harder to navigate. Bring your own flashlight (farms often run out of loaners) and check the farm's rules: some allow flashlights, some only glow sticks, and a few run them fully dark.

Night mazes are best for older kids and adults. The corn seems taller in the dark, the turns come faster, and the sounds of the farm — an owl, a rustling stalk, someone screaming two rows over — do half the work for you.

## Finding your way out

The corn maze has a simple rule: **pick a wall and follow it.** If you always turn the same direction at every intersection, you will eventually walk the entire perimeter and find the exit. It is not the fastest route, but it works.

For a faster exit, use the checkpoints. The stations are numbered in order, so following them in sequence keeps you moving generally toward the exit rather than in circles.

And if you truly get lost? Most farms have staff on hand and a "help" flag or phone number printed on the map. It is a field of corn, not the woods — you are never far from the edge.
